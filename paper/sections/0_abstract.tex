Vision--language models (VLMs) learn rich image--text associations, yet they often attend to the wrong part of the image: the visual attention behind a generated word is noisy and tends to fall on background or image borders rather than the object the word names. We introduce the \emph{Saliency Alignment Loss}, a simple auxiliary objective that teaches a VLM to look where it is describing. As the model generates a caption, we pull the attention behind each grounded word toward the image region that word refers to, supervised by dense grounding annotations. The loss adds no parameters and no architectural changes, and drops into an ordinary fine-tuning run. Its effect is large and immediate: after only $200$ steps on LLaVA-1.5-7B, the share of attention landing on the named object rises $4.6{\times}$, with matching gains on two complementary localization measures, while general question answering and hallucination behavior are left intact. We also pinpoint \emph{where} the model changes: the language model, not the vision--language projector, absorbs the signal, and does so consistently across runs, concentrating the update in a small set of attention heads. Finally, we are clear about the method's limits: this much sharper attention does \emph{not}, on its own, raise downstream benchmark scores, evidence that attention localization is a capability worth supervising directly rather than a free path to better answers. We outline an instruction-tuned variant designed to turn these localization gains into downstream ones.
